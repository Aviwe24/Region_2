Urban Design 1 · Studio session

Building in the Middle

A three-hour studio on missing middle housing that starts each group’s Vrededorp typologies panel. First-year students read, draw and measure eight housing types, adapt them to Vrededorp stands, and argue where they belong.

Typologies panel, A1

Between the freestanding house and the block of flats sits a range of small buildings that each hold several homes: semi-detached pairs, fourplexes, cottage courts, row houses, courtyard walk-ups and shophouses. Daniel Parolek of Opticos Design named these missing middle housing in 2010, because zoning in many American cities had stopped them being built after the 1940s.

Johannesburg tells a different story. Much of its middle already exists, in backyard rooms, walk-up flats, garden cottages and townhouse complexes, but a lot of it was built informally, behind walls, or under older rules. This session asks students to look at these buildings as urban designers: how each one meets the street, shares space and adds households while staying close to the scale of a house.

The students’ task is a typologies panel for Vrededorp, the inner-city suburb north-west of the CBD whose southern part, with neighbouring Pageview, was known as Fietas. Its small stands, its shopping street and the land cleared by forced removals make it a demanding site for the missing middle. The City’s Nodal Review asks for node-level density here, while the street asks for fine grain.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session, students can

  1. Explain what missing middle housing means as a built form, and how it differs from the South African “gap market” use of the term.
  2. Describe a housing type with a consistent set of attributes: stand, footprint, height, homes, access, shared space, parking and frontage.
  3. Draw a type at consistent scales (1:500 and 1:200) so it can be compared with other types.
  4. Calculate coverage, floor area ratio (FAR) and net density in dwelling units per hectare (du/ha) for a stand.
  5. Adapt a type to a Vrededorp stand and argue where it belongs, using the Nodal Review zones and what the building gives the street.
  6. Compose a typologies panel that compares a family of types at consistent scales.

Opener · 10 minutes · project this

What the street shows

Students see only the street elevation. Ask: how many households live on each stand? Collect guesses, then reveal the plans. The detached house and the fourplex look alike from the pavement, and the smallest stand holds the most households.

Mini-lecture · 25 minutes

Five ideas to teach

Talking points for the lecture, in teaching order. Each idea takes about five minutes. Project the lecture slides; they carry these points as speaker notes.

1

The middle of the housing spectrum

Parolek’s spectrum runs from the detached house to the mid-rise block. The types in between hold from two to about twenty homes but stay close to the size of a large house. Opticos describes them by a handful of shared traits:

  • House-scale: a footprint and height like a large house, rarely above three storeys.
  • Several homes per building, usually smaller than a typical house.
  • A walkable setting, close to shops, schools and public transport.
  • Lower perceived density: they look less dense than the numbers say.
  • Fewer parking bays, often no more than one per home.
  • Simple construction that small builders can manage.
  • Shared spaces where neighbours meet.

In Opticos-based guidance these types usually reach about 16–35 dwelling units per acre, roughly 40–85 du/ha net, depending on the type and the lot.

Street elevations at one scale, from the detached house to the mid-rise block. Scroll sideways to see the whole spectrum.

Point at the backyard yard. It is one storey high and reaches 200 du/ha. Height and density are different things.

2

Two meanings in South Africa

Built form

A family of building types between the house and the apartment block. This is Parolek’s meaning and the one this session uses.

Income band

The “gap market”: households who earn too much to qualify for a state-subsidised house and too little to get a home loan.

The two overlap. Small rental buildings are one of the main ways gap-market households find a home. Ask students to say which meaning they are using whenever the term comes up.

3

Johannesburg already builds a middle

  • Backyard rooms. In Soweto, Alexandra and other townships, owners build rooms and small flats in their yards. Urban LandMark called small-scale private rental one of the most successful and widespread housing systems in South Africa. Some owners, known as micro-developers, now build two-storey blocks of self-contained units, and specialist lenders finance them.
  • Walk-up flats. Blocks of three to five storeys without lifts, common in Yeoville, Berea and the inner city.
  • Garden cottages. The Land Use Scheme 2018 allows up to two subsidiary dwelling units on a Residential 1 stand, with a combined floor area of no more than 160 m² or 90% of the main house, whichever is smaller.
  • Townhouse complexes. Rows of attached houses, very often in gated complexes facing an internal road.
  • Semi-detached houses. Found in older suburbs and in some subsidised housing projects.

The urban design problem. Much of this middle turns its back on the street. Walls, palisade fences, single gates and internal roads mean that adding households does not always add life to the street. So ask two questions of every building: how many homes, and what does it give the street?

4

Typology as an urban designer’s method

A type is the shared organisation behind many buildings: how homes, entrances, open space and parking are arranged on a stand. A typology is the system you use to sort and compare types. Urban designers draw types at the same scale and describe them with the same attributes, so that differences show up. Six lenses give students the same questions for every type:

FrontageWhat does the building show the street: doors and windows, or a wall?
ThresholdWhat lies between pavement and front door: a stoep, a garden, a gate, a courtyard?
AccessHow many front doors are there, and where are they?
Shared spaceWhat is shared, and who looks after it?
Parking and serviceWhere do cars and bins go, and what does that cost the street?
Grain and scaleDoes it keep the rhythm of the street’s stand widths and heights?
5

Measuring density

Five numbers describe a type on its stand. Here they are for the fourplex on a 500 m² stand:

Coveragefootprint ÷ stand area = 200 ÷ 500 = 40%
Gross floor area (GFA)footprint × storeys = 200 × 2 = 400 m²
Floor area ratio (FAR)GFA ÷ stand area = 400 ÷ 500 = 0.8
Net densityhomes ÷ stand area in ha = 4 ÷ 0.05 = 80 du/ha
Open space per home(stand − footprint − parking) ÷ homes = (500 − 200 − 50) ÷ 4 ≈ 63 m²
  • 1 hectare is 10,000 m², a square 100 m by 100 m. Divide m² by 10,000 to get hectares.
  • Net density counts the stand only. Gross density adds streets, parks and other public land, so it is always lower.
  • Density is not crowding. Density counts homes per hectare; crowding counts people per room.
  • The same density can take very different forms. Berghauser Pont and Haupt’s Spacematrix shows this across whole neighbourhoods.

The site · Region F

Vrededorp

The panels are for Vrededorp, north-west of the CBD beside Pageview and Fordsburg and close to Braamfontein. Share this section with groups before they start drawing.

A short history

  1. 1890s

    Laid out on the edge of the young mining town; the name means “village of peace”. Most of the suburb, north of 11th Street, housed poor white families, many of them tenants.

  2. 1900s–1950s

    South of 11th Street and in the neighbouring Malay Location, renamed Pageview in 1943, Malay, Indian and Coloured families, among others, built a mixed community known as Fietas. 14th Street became one of the city’s busiest shopping streets.

  3. 1960s–1970s

    Under the Group Areas Act the area was declared white. Residents were evicted and forcibly removed, many homes were bulldozed, and large parts of the land were never rebuilt.

  4. 2020–2022

    The Johannesburg Development Agency ran a revitalisation programme for Vrededorp and Pageview’s public spaces, under the Empire–Perth Strategic Area Framework.

  5. Today

    The Fietas Museum at 25 14th Street, a provincial heritage site, and the 23rd Street Mosque survive among newer buildings and vacant stands.

What the site gives the panel

Stands
Small. Many stands sold today are about 250 m², and historically one stand often held several cottages. Measure real stands from the City’s GIS or an aerial photo.
14th Street
Rows of one- and two-storey buildings with shops below, homes above and colonnaded verandas over the pavement.
Grain
Alleys, backyards and stoeps between the buildings.
Cleared land
Sites left empty after the removals, open to infill.
Policy
Vrededorp sits on the line between a Metropolitan Node (at least 80 du/ha, 3 to 20 storeys) and the Inner City Node (at least 100 du/ha, 3 storeys and up).

Four tensions for the panel

Density and grain80 du/ha on a 250 m² stand is only two homes. Three storeys on a small stand is the harder ask.
MemoryWhat does a new building owe 14th Street and the families removed from Fietas?
Who it housesFamilies, tenants, students near Braamfontein. Who gains a home, and who could be pushed out?
Street lifeVerandas, shopfronts and stoeps, or walls and gates?

A place of forced removal. Treat the Fietas Museum and residents’ stories as sources alongside maps. Avoid calling cleared land empty; ask who lived there, and who lives there now.

Studio 1today · 50 min

Typology 1

Take your type card and your Vrededorp stand. Fit the type to the stand, then draw on one A3 sheet:

  1. Plan at 1:200. Homes, entrances, stairs, shared space and parking.
  2. Street section at 1:200. From the opposite pavement to the back of the stand. Show everything between the pavement and a front door.
  3. Massing sketch. A quick axonometric or a card model.
  4. Metrics box. Stand area, footprint, storeys, GFA, homes, coverage, FAR, net density and open space per home, with working.

Check it in the plot lab against Vrededorp’s targets: 80 or 100 du/ha, and three storeys. This sheet becomes panel column C.

Studio 2today · 35 min

The line-up

Pin your sheet on the density line at your net density. Walk the whole line and discuss:

  • Which types reach 80 du/ha on a Vrededorp stand, and at what height?
  • Which look denser than they are? Which look less dense?
  • Which give the most to the street, and which give the least?
  • Which of these would your own family live in? Why?
Studio 3today · 25 min

Place it in Vrededorp

Choose a real place for your type: 14th Street, a residential street north of 11th Street, or a cleared site. Prepare a 60-second pitch:

“Our type belongs on street in Vrededorp, because it reaches density at storeys, it gives the street frontage, threshold or shared space, and it keeps something of Fietas.”

Nodal Review map

The City’s Nodal Review, approved by Council in February 2020, sorts Johannesburg into zones that guide where and how densely to build. This map is drawn from the NodalReview.json and Regions.json files in this repository. Letters mark the seven administrative regions, and the ring marks Vrededorp, shown close up below the map. Select a zone to see its guidance and a prompt for the pitch.

Density and height figures come from City and press summaries of the policy. The policy also asks for at least 60 du/ha within 500 m of Rea Vaya, Gautrain and PRASA stations. Check all figures against the approved document before you teach. Common misconceptions

“Missing middle means middle-income housing.”
That is the gap-market meaning. Today the term is about built form.
“Density means tall buildings.”
The backyard yard reaches 200 du/ha at one storey.
“High density means overcrowding.”
Density counts homes per hectare. Crowding counts people per room.
Mixing net and gross density.
Today every figure is net: the stand only.
Forgetting to convert to hectares.
Divide m² by 10,000 before dividing homes by area.
